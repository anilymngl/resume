A note on the work, and who it was with.

The résumé version is clean; this one is true.

The short answer is that I build applied AI systems and evaluation workflows on governed data platforms. The longer answer is that the work has almost never been solo. Forecasting a retailer's stock is a conversation with a merchandiser. Scoring a Cortex Analyst answer is a conversation with the analyst who cared enough to say this is wrong, and here is why. What I have learned, across nine years, is that the engineering is the easy part.

Staying legible to the people who use the work — the merchandiser on Monday, the analyst on Friday, the teammate who will read the pull request next week — is the part that takes practice. I am still practicing.

the work, briefly

2017 – 2022
Invent Analytics. Retail optimization, forecasting, replenishment, production decision logic. Multiple clients, squads of two to four.
2022 – 2025
BlueCloud, for JLL Technologies. Snowflake & Databricks pipelines, governed metrics, schema standards, reliability.
2026 –
BlueCloud, AI/ML. Cortex Analyst and Cortex Search evaluation frameworks, plus the Semantic View Factory.
ongoing
Local prototypes. Workbenches, not products. RAG Glassbox, Cognos Studio, Signal Ledger.

with, & learned from

  • My Invent squads, 2017 – 2022. Who taught me that an optimization is only as good as the person who has to trust it on Monday morning.
  • The BlueCloud data team, 2022 – present. Who taught me that reliability is the most underrated form of respect.
  • Collaborators on the AI/ML side. Who push back when an evaluation looks too clean to be real.
  • The quiet maintainers. dbt, Streamlit, TruLens, PySpark, Pandas, countless others — tools I used today and will use tomorrow, built by people I will never meet.
  • Everyone who has been senior to me, and everyone who has been junior. I am still learning both directions.

practice, in small things

  • Credit people by name when the work was not mine alone.
  • Optimize for the person reading the code next, not for looking clever.
  • Push back early; apologize later only if it is earned.
  • Let the women in the room finish their sentences.
  • Mentor quietly. Loud mentorship is usually about the mentor.
  • Ask more questions on day one than on day one hundred.
  • Say I don't know, out loud, when I don't.

— Anıl, from Istanbul, with warmth.
